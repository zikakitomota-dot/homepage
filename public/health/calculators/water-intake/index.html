Water Intake Calculator

Create a rough planning estimate for total daily water from body weight and exercise. This is not a prescription or a requirement to drink the displayed amount as plain water.

Planning estimate for total daily water

—

    What this estimate does—and does not mean

    This tool uses a simple planning rule rather than a clinical hydration assessment. It calculates a body-weight baseline, then adds a fixed allowance for the exercise minutes entered:

    Baseline = weight (kg) × 35 ml

    Exercise allowance = 350 ml for every 30 minutes entered

    Displayed estimate = baseline + exercise allowance

    The 35 ml/kg and fixed exercise allowance are common consumer-planning heuristics; they are not National Academies requirements and are not tailored to sweat rate. The result should be read as a rough comparison point, not a precise amount your body needs.

    How it compares with dietary reference values

    The US National Academies reports adult adequate intakes for total water of about 3.7 litres per day for men and 2.7 litres per day for women. Those population reference values include water from all beverages and moisture in food. They are adequate intakes based on observed intakes, not exact daily requirements for each individual.

    This calculator does not ask about sex, food moisture, climate, pregnancy, breastfeeding, medication, or health conditions. A result above or below those population values is therefore not, by itself, evidence of dehydration or excessive intake.

    Worked example and practical interpretation

    Example: 70 kg with 30 minutes of exercise.

    Baseline = 70 × 35 = 2,450 ml

    Exercise allowance = 350 ml

    Planning estimate = 2,800 ml (2.8 litres)

    The 2.8-litre result means total water across the day, including ordinary drinks and water contained in food. It does not mean drinking 2.8 litres of plain water in addition to everything else.

    Why individual needs vary

    Weather, altitude, exercise duration and intensity, clothing, body size, pregnancy, breastfeeding, illness, fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, medicines, and kidney or heart conditions can all change fluid needs. During exercise, sweat loss also varies widely between people. The American College of Sports Medicine therefore recommends individualised replacement rather than one fixed exercise amount for everyone.

    For an ordinary day, thirst, meals, and normal access to drinks help guide intake for many healthy adults. For prolonged exercise or hot conditions, a personal sweat-rate estimate based on body weight before and after a representative session can be more informative than the fixed allowance used here.

    When not to rely on this calculator

    • Do not use it to manage a medical fluid restriction or a condition affecting fluid balance.
    • Do not use it as an endurance-event hydration plan or to replace electrolytes.
    • Do not force water quickly to reach the displayed number.
    • Follow a qualified healthcare professional's instructions when they differ from this estimate.

    Methodology and sources

    This calculator produces an estimate from the method described on this page. The references below document the underlying formula, comparison values, or important limitations; they do not turn the result into an individual medical recommendation.

    • National Academies — Dietary Reference Intakes for Water — explains adequate intake as total water from beverages and food, not a precise requirement for every person.
    • American College of Sports Medicine — Exercise and Fluid Replacement — emphasises that sweat losses vary and that exercise fluid replacement should be individualised.

    Frequently asked questions

    How much water should I drink a day?

    There is no single exact amount for every adult. This calculator uses a 35 ml/kg planning baseline, but the National Academies' adequate-intake values describe total water from food and drinks and are not individual prescriptions.

    Does other fluid and food count?

    Yes. Water in tea, coffee, milk, and foods such as fruit and soup all contribute to your daily fluid intake, so you rarely need to drink the full amount as plain water.

    Can I drink too much water?

    Yes. Drinking excessive amounts quickly can be dangerous. Do not force fluids to reach a calculator number, and seek individual advice if illness, medication, endurance exercise, or a clinician's fluid instructions apply to you.

    Is this a medical recommendation?

    No. This is a general estimate for healthy adults. If you have kidney, heart, or other conditions that affect fluid balance, follow the advice of your healthcare provider.
