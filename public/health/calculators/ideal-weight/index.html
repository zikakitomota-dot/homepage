Reference Weight Calculator

Compare three historical height-based reference equations, sometimes called “ideal weight” formulas. The range is not a medically required weight, personal health target, or diagnosis.

Estimated reference weight

—

This is a height-based reference estimate, not a single medically prescribed or “perfect” weight.

    Different formulas use slightly different assumptions, so their estimates can vary. Metric results are shown in kilograms; imperial results are shown in pounds.

    These estimates do not directly account for muscle mass, body composition, frame size, pregnancy, medical conditions, or your individual health context.

    What a Reference-Weight Estimate Means

    A reference weight calculator provides a historical height-based estimate, not a definitive health target. These equations are often described as “ideal weight” formulas in search and reference material, but they originated in actuarial height–weight tables and drug-dosage work. They were not designed to identify one medically required or personally ideal weight for every adult. This tool compares three traditional formulas and displays the lowest and highest estimates as a range.

    Terms such as “ideal,” “healthy,” and “perfect” weight are sometimes used interchangeably, but no single number can describe the right weight for every person. The result is best used as a starting point for context.

    How Height Is Used to Estimate Reference Weight

    Height is the main driver in all three formulas. Each starts with a base weight at 5 feet (152.4 cm) and adjusts that estimate for additional height, with separate constants for the male and female selections.

    Height alone does not determine health. The formulas do not directly measure body composition, muscle mass, or frame size, so their results should not be treated as a diagnosis or prescribed weight goal.

    How the Historical Reference-Weight Formulas Work

    The calculator uses three established reference methods. Their different base weights and height adjustments explain why the results are not identical:

    Robinson: 52 kg (men) / 49 kg (women) + 1.9 / 1.7 kg per inch over 5 ft

    Devine: 50 kg (men) / 45.5 kg (women) + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 ft

    Miller: 56.2 kg (men) / 53.1 kg (women) + 1.41 / 1.36 kg per inch over 5 ft

    All calculations are performed in kilograms. Imperial mode converts each result to pounds for display without changing the underlying formula.

    Example Calculation

    Person: a man 178 cm tall (about 70.1 inches, or 10.1 inches over 5 ft).

    Devine = 50 + 2.3 × 10.1 ≈ 73.2 kg

    Robinson = 52 + 1.9 × 10.1 ≈ 71.1 kg

    Miller = 56.2 + 1.41 × 10.1 ≈ 70.4 kg

    The three formulas produce a reference range of roughly 70.4–73.2 kg. The variation reflects their different assumptions rather than an error.

    Does Age Affect the Result?

    Most traditional reference-weight formulas, including the three used here, are based on height and sex rather than age. This calculator therefore does not adjust its estimate directly for age.

    Age may still matter in a broader assessment of health, body composition, and personal circumstances. It simply is not part of these particular equations.

    How to Interpret the Reference Range

    Use the range as an educational comparison rather than a required target. It does not directly account for muscle mass, body composition, frame size, health history, pregnancy, medical conditions, or other individual context.

    For a separate height-and-current-weight screening estimate, use the BMI Calculator. To explore resting and total daily energy needs, use the BMR Calculator or TDEE Calculator.

    Why the Result Should Not Become a Target

    These equations were created for historical reference and dosing contexts, not to prescribe one body weight for every adult. A narrow spread between the three answers only means the formulas happen to agree for that height; it does not make the result more personal or medically correct.

    If weight is relevant to a health decision, consider current health, body composition, history, and professional advice rather than trying to reach the calculated range simply because it appears on screen.

    Methodology and sources

    This calculator produces an estimate from the method described on this page. The references below document the underlying formula, comparison values, or important limitations; they do not turn the result into an individual medical recommendation.

    • Robinson et al. (1983) — Ideal body weight for drug-dosage calculations — documents the origin and intended drug-dosage context of the Robinson equation.

    Frequently asked questions

    What does this reference weight calculator estimate?

    It compares historical height-based equations, often called ideal-weight formulas, using your height and sex. The result is a general reference range, not a medically required weight or personal health target.

    What is a healthy weight for my height?

    Height is useful for estimating reference weights, but it cannot determine health by itself. Body composition, muscle mass, frame size, medical context, and other factors also matter. A BMI calculation can provide a separate height-and-weight screening measure.

    Why do the reference-weight formulas give different results?

    Robinson, Devine, and Miller use different starting weights and adjustments for each inch above 5 feet. Their estimates therefore vary slightly, which is why the calculator presents the lowest and highest results as a reference range.

    Does age affect this reference-weight calculation?

    No. These traditional formulas are based primarily on height and sex rather than age, so this calculator does not adjust its result directly for age. Age can still be relevant to an individual health assessment.

    Is a reference-weight estimate the same as BMI?

    No. These historical formulas estimate a reference weight from height and sex. BMI uses both your current weight and height to produce a separate adult weight-status screening value.

    Is there one perfect weight?

    No. There is no universally perfect weight for everyone of the same height. Treat the displayed range as an educational reference rather than a single correct target.

    Should I use kilograms or pounds?

    Use whichever measurement system is most familiar. Metric mode shows results in kilograms, while imperial mode shows the same formula estimates converted to pounds.
